overview

Rhiza Research is a nonprofit that seeks out data, technology, and technical capacity gaps and develops creative, open, and accessible technical solutions to fill those gaps. We are dedicated to working on projects that we believe will have a positive impact on the well-being of ecosystems and communities, from electric grids to weather forecasting, from ecology to music.

sheerwater benchmark

Agriculture-first evaluation of the world's leading AI forecasting, NWP, and satellite products.

Public release in early 2027. Please reach out for early access.

Weather and climate forecasts are evaluated far more rigorously in North America and Europe than in Africa, even though the stakes of forecast accuracy are often highest for the smallholder farmers, pastoralists, and communities who depend on seasonal rains. Sheerwater is Rhiza Research's response to that gap: an open-source library for benchmarking machine-learning and physics-based weather and climate forecasts with an explicit focus on model performance across the African continent. Rather than treating global average skill scores as good enough, Sheerwater surfaces how forecasts actually perform in the places where reliable information is scarcest and most consequential — starting with an initial focus on East Africa and partnerships in Kenya.

The project provides a common set of evaluation metrics and a validation interface for comparing forecast systems against ground-truth observations from satellite, reanalysis, and station-based sources, spanning nearly three decades of data at multiple spatial and temporal resolutions. Because it's built to be extensible, researchers and forecast developers can plug in their own models for standardized, apples-to-apples comparison rather than relying on one-off validation studies.

Sheerwater is developed with support from the Gates Foundation and a group of academic and industry collaborators, and builds on earlier research in subseasonal forecast benchmarking. The code is at github.com/rhiza-research/sheerwater.

Weather skills turn scientific weather-data pipelines into fixed, reviewable tools for agentic AI. You ask in plain language; the agent picks skills and runs them in order — fetch a forecast or satellite product, clip it to a region, aggregate it in time, plot the result. Each output records which skill, version, and arguments produced it, so someone else can rerun or check the chain. The skill is the script that was checked in; nothing is generated at runtime unless you want it to be.

Initiated by Rhiza Research. The live catalog is at weather-skills.org. The code is at github.com/rhiza-research/forecasting-skills.

the power equality initiative

Over the past decades, sweeping efforts have been made to ensure global access to electricity. However, in much of the world, even those with access to electricity still receive electricity with poor reliability and quality. This lack of quality undermines the proposed benefits of electrification in homes, businesses, schools, and hospitals and also makes it much more difficult for utilities to transition their grids to cleaner energy.

The Power EQuality Initiative aims to highlight and act on the global disparity of access to high-quality electricity. Rhiza Research is building a community to address the problem of power inequality, hosting a series of lectures, and directly working to create an open power quality analysis toolkit to increase the accessibility and impact of power quality measurement worldwide. We will roll out an open-source power quality measurement device, release a library of high-resolution standards-compliant power quality data, and work with manufacturers to integrate measurement capabilities into existing grid-connected devices like smart meters and inverters.

SEER

Sustainable Electricity Expansion Roadmaps

Power systems planning determines how countries meet growing electricity demand with cost-effective, sustainable investments in generation and grid infrastructure — but today it's out of reach for many of the places that need it most. Conventional planning relies on expensive, specialized software and proprietary datasets, limiting most countries to a handful of scenarios updated only once every five to ten years. This is especially true in the Global South, where the lack of accessible planning data and tools slows the transition to renewable energy and risks locking in decades of fossil-fuel infrastructure.

SEER is building the first open, machine-learning-ready dataset for power systems planning, covering 15+ countries with a focus on low-resource settings where planning capacity is most constrained. The project curates ground-truth planning inputs and outputs — demand curves, cost parameters, and remotely sensed grid structures — from existing national studies, and expands them into 75,000 training scenarios that can power fast, ML-based planning tools in place of slow, costly conventional solvers. Paired with an interactive visualization platform, SEER aims to make robust, frequently updated planning accessible to energy planners and policymakers everywhere.

Funded by Climate Change AI, in collaboration with the University of Washington — Dr. June Lukuyu and Ahana Mukherjee.

abc and pbc subseasonal forecasting

Winner of the 2026 WMO and ECMWF AI Weather Quest, first for every weather variable and lead time.

Subseasonal forecasts — weather two to six weeks ahead — sit in a hard gap between ordinary weather prediction and seasonal climate outlooks. They are the timescale that matters for planting, water allocation, and preparing for extremes, and they are also where both physics-based and AI models lose skill to systematic bias.

Adaptive Bias Correction (ABC) is a machine-learning method that corrects those errors in dynamical forecasts of temperature and precipitation, combining operational models with observations. Probabilistic Bias Correction (PBC) extends the same idea to full forecast distributions, so it can improve both dynamical and AI ensembles. Applied to ECMWF’s leading systems, PBC doubled the subseasonal skill of the AI Forecasting System.

PBC — Advancing Subseasonal Forecasting with Machine Learning — arXiv

Kenya OND forecasts

Smallholder farmers across sub-Saharan Africa have the forecasts they need—backed by their national meteorological and hydrological services—to make critical agricultural decisions during the rainy season.

Kenya’s long rains (March–May) and short rains (October–December) are the difference between a harvest and a loss for millions of smallholder farmers. National meteorological services already produce strong weekly weather and seasonal climate outlooks; the hard gap is the weeks in between — onset, early-season dry spells, and whether a wet start will hold.

Rhiza collaborates with the Kenya Meteorological Service Authority (KMSA) and the Climate Hazards Center (CHC) at UC Santa Barbara in support of KMSA’s forecast production. Together we develop downscaled subseasonal rainfall forecasts, rainy-season onset products, and shared comparisons against satellite and station observations.

Developed in partnership with KMSA and CHC. Forecast products from the collaboration are published at kenya-forecasts.sheerwater.rhizaresearch.org.

weather station QC

Automated weather stations only help if you can tell good rain from a clogged gauge. Rhiza works with TAHMO on quality control for the TAHMO station network: flagging faults, cleaning daily precipitation, and producing an analysis-ready record that researchers and forecast developers can actually use.

That work produced TAHMO Cleaned Precipitation Data — daily observations that have been rigorously cleaned and quality-controlled for analysis and model evaluation. The dataset is suitable for validating satellite rainfall products, evaluating forecast skill, and studying wet spells, dry spells, and rainy-season onset.

Developed in partnership with TAHMO. The cleaned dataset is available from tahmo.org/climate-data.
